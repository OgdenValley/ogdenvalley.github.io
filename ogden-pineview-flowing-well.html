CASE FILE · PINEVIEW PIPELINE PROJECT · OC 012

Pineview's flowing well was reportedly capped. Here's what we could verify.

The drawdown for Ogden's pipeline project exposed a 1915 well that was still flowing. The state inspected it and recommended sealing it with cement from bottom to top. A Nov. 15 post said it was capped. We have not located a record showing who did the work, when, or how.

Water right 35-1983Well ID (WIN) 452337Drilled 1915 · 65 ftFlow: about 20.5 gal/min (Sept. 25)

Posted Nov. 19, 2026 · Updated Nov. 19, 2026

WHAT
A 1915 flowing well on the floor of Pineview Reservoir
  • Where: About 500 feet off Pineview's south shore, on a parcel the state describes as currently federal land, directly north of the Jensen-Fowers Farm (state memo, Sept. 28, 2026).
  • What the state found: No record that it was sealed before the reservoir filled in 1937. It was flowing about 20.5 gallons a minute on Sept. 25, 2026.
  • What the state recommended: Fill it with cement from the bottom (65 feet) to the surface, done by a licensed Utah well driller, before the reservoir refills in January 2027.
  • What was reported: Ogden Valley News posted on Nov. 15 that "the running artesian well on Pineview Reservoir bottom has been capped."

In plain words

When Pineview was drained for Ogden's pipeline project, an old well on the reservoir floor turned out to still be flowing. The state's Division of Water Rights inspected it in September. It said that if the water ever stops flowing up, the open pipe could let reservoir water run down into the groundwater, so it recommended filling the whole well with cement. On Nov. 15, a local news page said the well had been capped. We have not located a record of who did the work, when, how, or who paid. We also haven't confirmed that the well in the Nov. 15 post is the one the state inspected.

Explain it like I'm 10

  • More than 100 years ago, someone drilled a well where Pineview Reservoir is now. Water came up out of it by itself.
  • When the reservoir was built, the well ended up under the water, still open.
  • This year the reservoir was drained, and people could see the well again.
  • The state said: fill this old well all the way with cement so dirty water can't sneak down into the water underground.
  • Someone says it was capped. We're checking the records to see who did it and how.

The short version

  • The state memo: Division of Water Rights, Sept. 28, 2026, water right 35-1983 / WIN 452337. It recommends permanent abandonment with cement from bottom to top.
  • The report: Ogden Valley News, Nov. 15, 2026, said the well "has been capped."
  • The online state record (Nov. 17): WIN 452337 shows one activity, "New," dated 06/01/1915. There is no abandonment activity and no water-quality records.
  • Not located yet: who did the work, when, how, the abandonment log, UGS's water test results, and who paid.
  • Not confirmed: that the well in the Nov. 15 post is the one the state inspected.

Why a flowing old well matters

The Utah Geological Survey's 2019 Special Study 165 on Ogden Valley groundwater looked at old wells under Pineview. These quotes were read directly from the study (printed page numbers):

  • Page 68: "At least 51 wells were drilled in the Artesian Park area." Some were abandoned in the 1970s because of bacteria problems. "The state of most wells at abandonment is unknown and decades of further decay may have deteriorated plugs or caps." The authors say such wells "may now be a conduit between Pineview Reservoir and the confined aquifer," which feeds Ogden's drinking-water wells.
  • Page 96: A water-balance model "suggests that there must have been at least 2700 acre-feet of reservoir water moving to the confined aquifer in 2016."
  • The study's own caveats (p. 96): "Without more information, we are unable to positively identify and quantify the interaction between Pineview Reservoir and the confined aquifer." Stable isotopes "do not reflect reservoir influence." But tritium and elevated nitrate in an Ogden City well are "convincing evidence that recent groundwater recharge is entering the confined aquifer."

Ogden City's pipeline project website says the water for the city's wellfield comes from a confined aquifer that is separated from Pineview Reservoir and is not affected by reservoir levels.

These statements answer different questions. The city is addressing whether the drawdown will interrupt its wellfield supply. UGS identified possible paths by which shallower water could reach the confined aquifer. The study does not establish that this exposed well is one of those paths. The state memo raises the same kind of concern about the well it inspected, namely what could happen if its flow stops. It does not report test results.

Background: Ogden's old Artesian Park wells

This part is regional history. It does not establish who owned or is responsible for the well in the memo. The memo associates that well with water right 35-1983, Jensen-Fowers Farm LLC, and identifies Simon Jensen as its original owner.

  • U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Ogden River Project history: "The artesian wells owned by Ogden City were capped and abandoned."
  • Ogden Valley News, December 1999 (p. 20, compiled by Shanna Francis), quoting a September 1984 Standard-Examiner article: "Under a contract with Ogden, the Bureau of Reclamation took over responsibility for the artesian system."
  • Standard-Examiner, Sept. 18, 2026: Justin Anderson, Ogden City Public Services Executive Director, said that when the dam was raised in 1970, "our wells all got capped underneath Pineview Reservoir."
  • UGS Special Study 165, p. 6: before the reservoir filled, Ogden City "plugged some of the wells and extended others to higher ground."

What state rules say about sealing a well

  • Who does the work (R655-4-14): Abandoning a well must be done under the direct supervision of a currently licensed water well driller, who is "responsible for verification of the procedures and materials used."
  • Notice before (R655-4-4, 4.2.5): The driller must notify the state engineer before starting. No start card is needed. The notice can go by phone, fax or email.
  • Record after (R655-4-4, 4.6.2): An abandonment log is due "within 30 days of the completion of abandonment work."
  • Artesian wells (14.7): A cement or concrete plug goes in the confining layer "so as to prevent subsurface leakage from the artesian zone." The rest is filled with grout or bentonite.
  • Temporary caps (14.1): A well temporarily removed from service needs a tamper-resistant, watertight seal. The rule also limits temporary abandonment during construction to 90 days. How this applies to a 1915 well on a reservoir floor is a question for the Division of Water Rights.

The historic-discovery condition. The same state notice says the project does not "affect any cultural or historical properties." It also says that if anything with possible cultural or historic significance is uncovered during construction, Ogden City "will be required to stop construction and notify both the Division of Drinking Water (the Division) and the State Historic Preservation Officer." KSL reported the exposed century-old well structures on Oct. 13, and the notice was posted Oct. 27. This does not establish that the condition was triggered or missed.

2. The collective view: transparency, financial accountability, environmental impact

  • Environmental impact: The memo's concern is contamination if the upward flow stops. Sealing the well properly can serve the public good. Whether it does depends on how it was sealed.
  • Transparency: The state inspected the well and wrote up a recommendation, and Ogden City posted that memo on its project website. The closure itself became public through a Facebook post. We have not located a record of who did the work, when, or how.
  • Financial accountability: We have not located a record showing who paid.
  • Done in the open: The pipeline project has had public briefings, a project website and regular local news updates.

3. The legislative view

  • Repealed in 2010: Utah Code 73-2-21 gave the state engineer power to "plug, repair, or to otherwise control artesian wells which are wasting public water." H.B. 69 (2010), "State Engineer's Plugging of Wells Repealer," repealed it. Its primary sponsor was Rep. James R. Gowans, and its Senate sponsor was Sen. David P. Hinkins. The enrolled bill gives no reason.
  • Still on the books: Utah Code 73-5-9 lets the state engineer require plugging of caps, valves or casings on wells to prevent waste or contamination, paid for by the owner, lessee or person in control.
  • Who is responsible? The memo associates the well with water right 35-1983, Jensen-Fowers Farm LLC, on a parcel it describes as currently federal land. That alone doesn't establish current ownership or responsibility. The memo's word is "recommend," not "order."
  • Rule gap: Nothing in the abandonment rules reviewed requires public notice when an old well on public land is sealed.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

Use water right 35-1983 and WIN 452337 when you ask.

  3. Ogden City Council: If the city paid, was it under the pipeline contract, by change order, or separately?
